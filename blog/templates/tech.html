What a Water Flow Test Taught Me Before a Metal Print

I printed our injector in resin, ran water through it, and measured what it actually flowed. The numbers disagreed with the CAD, and that disagreement saved a metal print.

Metal printing an injector is slow and expensive. Water is free. So before sending the file for our LX-1 injector off to be printed in metal, I printed it in resin, bolted it to a garden-hose rig and measured how much water it actually flowed.

The short version: the injector flowed less than the CAD said it should. Not by a disaster, but by enough that the engine would have run rich of where we designed it.

01Why test with water first

An injector is mostly holes. Their size, angle and finish decide how much propellant goes through at a given pressure drop, and small errors compound. A cold-flow test with water is the cheapest way to find out if the holes you drew are the holes you got.

A water test gives you a discharge coefficient, not a combustion result. It says nothing about mixing or stability. It only tells you how the geometry flows.

02The rig

Nothing fancy. Everything came from the lab shelf or a hardware store near Dilshad Garden.

  • SupplyMains water through a regulator, so the pressure holds steady for each run.
  • PressureOne transducer just upstream of the injector, logged over serial.
  • FlowA catch bucket on a kitchen scale and a stopwatch. Crude, repeatable.

03Computing Cd

For each run you know the mass caught, the time, and the pressure drop. Divide the real mass flow by the ideal one and you get Cd:

cd.pypython 
import numpy as np RHO = 998.0          # water, kg/m^3D_ORIFICE = 0.8e-3   # m, as drawn in CAD def discharge_coeff(mass_kg, t_s, dp_pa, n_holes):    """Cd from a timed catch at a steady pressure drop."""    mdot = mass_kg / t_s    area = n_holes * np.pi * (D_ORIFICE / 2) ** 2    ideal = area * np.sqrt(2 * RHO * dp_pa)    return mdot / ideal

Every run landed below the Cd the design assumed. Printed holes come out a touch undersized and rough-walled, and resin and metal both do it. The CAD was drawn for perfect holes.

04What changed in the print

Instead of trusting the drawing, I resized the orifices from the measured Cd so the metal part would flow what the engine needs. Then I re-printed the resin copy at the new size and ran the rig again before sending anything out. It matched.

One extra resin print and an afternoon with a hose, versus a metal part that would have been wrong on arrival. Easy trade.

05Takeaways

  1. Test the geometry you got, not the geometry you drew.
  2. Cheap rigs are fine if they are repeatable. Log every run.
  3. Put the measured Cd back into the model before the next print.
